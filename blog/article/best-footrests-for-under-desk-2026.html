Best Footrests for Under Desk 2026

An ergonomic footrest improves posture, reduces back strain, and increases comfort at your desk. We found the best footrests for home and office use in 2026.

By Sarah Mitchell ·May 7, 2026 ·10 min read

Poor leg and foot positioning at a desk is a primary contributor to lower back pain, hip strain, and poor posture. When your feet don't rest comfortably flat on the floor — because the desk is too high, your chair is set too tall to reach the keyboard, or your legs are shorter than average — the resulting posture strains the lumbar spine, compresses the thighs against the chair edge, and reduces circulation to the lower legs. A quality footrest eliminates that postural gap. Footrests raise your feet to the correct height, allowing your knees to sit at approximately 90 degrees with thighs roughly parallel to the floor. This restores the natural lumbar curve, reduces pressure on the spine and thighs, and allows the hip flexors to relax. The best footrests go beyond a simple platform — they offer height and angle adjustability to match different chair and desk heights, anti-slip surfaces to keep feet secure, and some include rocking or massage features that actively improve circulation during long sitting sessions. For this guide, we tested and evaluated six under-desk footrests across home office and professional environments, assessing height range, adjustability, platform size, surface grip, stability, and long-term comfort. We prioritized footrests that genuinely address the ergonomic gap rather than simply adding height, and we included options across foam, plastic, and metal construction to match different user preferences and budgets.

Buying Guide

Height and Angle Adjustment

The most important feature in a footrest is adjustability. Fixed-height footrests only work for users at a specific chair and desk combination. Adjustable-height footrests — typically offering 3–5 preset heights or continuously adjustable mechanisms — accommodate different users, chair heights, and desk heights. Angle adjustment matters too: a flat footrest works for some, but many users prefer a slight incline (10–30 degrees) that maintains a natural ankle angle. Some premium footrests offer independent control of height and angle, letting you dial in the perfect combination. When selecting a footrest, start by measuring the gap between the floor and your feet when seated at your optimal keyboard and monitor height — that measurement determines the minimum height range you need. The adjustment mechanism matters as much as the range available. Click-button height presets are the most common and durable — they lock reliably and don't slip under load. Continuously adjustable slide mechanisms offer more precision but can slip under the pressure of a heavy user's feet over time. For a shared workstation with multiple users, preset mechanisms are more practical since each user can snap to their preferred position quickly. Always buy a footrest with at least 2 more inches of range than your measured gap to allow room for experimentation and to account for different footwear heights across seasons.

Platform Size and Foot Coverage

Footrests range from small kidney-shaped platforms supporting only the heels and balls of feet to large full-length platforms covering the entire foot from heel to toe. Larger platforms allow more foot position variety — you can shift your feet, change your stance, or place one foot up and one flat on the floor. For users who alternate sitting positions throughout the day, an extended platform is worth the extra space under the desk. Narrower footrests are less obtrusive and can be tucked away when not needed. Width matters for dual-monitor or shared desk setups — measure the available floor space under your desk before purchasing a wide footrest. Platform depth (front-to-back measurement) is often overlooked but critical for taller users with larger feet. A platform that is too short forces the heel off the back edge or the toes off the front, creating the same imbalanced posture a footrest is meant to correct. Standard platforms run 11–14 inches in depth, adequate for up to approximately a size 12 US men's shoe. For larger feet, look for platforms of 15 inches or more. If both feet need to fit side by side, ensure width is at least 16 inches to allow comfortable, natural shoulder-width foot positioning.

Surface Texture: Non-Slip and Massage Features

Footrest surfaces range from smooth plastic to textured rubber to massage nubs. Smooth surfaces allow shoes to slide — acceptable for users who keep shoes on throughout the workday. Non-slip rubber or textured surfaces grip socked feet and shoes, making them better for active foot repositioning. Massage footrests with nodule surfaces stimulate pressure points on the sole — a genuine circulation benefit for users who experience foot fatigue or swelling during long sitting periods. Rocking or tilting footrests allow feet to move through their natural range of motion rather than staying static, which improves circulation and reduces muscle fatigue from completely static positioning. Surface texture also affects noise, which matters in quiet office environments. Hard smooth plastic that allows shoe movement can produce a rubbing or scuffing sound as feet shift — a consideration in open-plan offices. Soft rubber or foam surfaces are near-silent even with frequent repositioning. For users wearing leather-soled shoes, a lightly textured rubber surface provides enough grip without catching. Massage nodule surfaces can be uncomfortable for users with plantar fasciitis or foot sensitivities — in those cases, a smooth padded foam surface is preferable. Washable covers on foam footrests add hygiene benefit, particularly for socked-foot use at shared workstations.

Material and Durability

Footrests are built from three main materials. Plastic shell footrests are the most common — lightweight, easy to clean, and inexpensive. Quality varies enormously: cheap thin plastic cracks or deforms under consistent pressure, while quality ABS or polycarbonate construction is durable enough for years of daily use. Foam footrests (memory foam or EVA foam) provide cushioning for socked-foot use — comfortable but they wear faster than hard surfaces. Metal footrests in steel or aluminum are the most durable but heaviest and typically the most expensive. For professional office environments where the footrest will see years of daily use, invest in quality plastic or metal construction. Load capacity is a closely related durability factor that manufacturers often underreport. Most footrests list a maximum supported weight — typically 220 to 400 lbs — but that specification reflects static load capacity, not dynamic load. A footrest used actively, with feet shifting, rocking, and pressing from different angles, experiences greater effective load than static weight alone suggests. Heavier users above 250 lbs should specifically seek footrests rated at 350 lbs or more and prefer metal or heavy-duty ABS plastic over basic plastic shells. Foam footrests in particular compress significantly faster under higher loads, losing their ergonomic height benefit within months rather than years of use.

Rocker vs Fixed Footrests

Static footrests hold feet in a fixed position at a set height. While better than no footrest, they don't address the circulation problems of completely static positioning. Rocker footrests tilt forward and backward, allowing feet to move through a rocking motion during the workday — activating calf muscles, improving blood flow, and reducing fidgeting that leads to foot tapping. Active footrests go further: some tilt in multiple directions, and some incorporate a balance board-style platform. For users who find static seating uncomfortable or have circulatory issues, an active rocker footrest is significantly more beneficial. For users with balance concerns, a static non-slip platform is safer. The practical difference between rocker and static footrests becomes most apparent during work sessions of four hours or more. For short sessions under two hours, a quality static footrest is sufficient — the circulatory benefit of rocking is mainly relevant to sustained, extended sitting. Budget also plays a role: rocker footrests cost more to manufacture and test for stability, with a typical price premium of $10–$20 over comparable static models. Many hybrid footrests offer a rocking function that can be locked flat — these are worth the modest extra cost if you are unsure which mode you'll prefer. Some users find the rocking motion distracting rather than beneficial, so testing before committing long-term is worthwhile when possible.

Anti-Slip Base and Stability

A footrest that slides around the floor defeats its purpose and creates a safety hazard. Look for footrests with substantial rubber non-slip bases that grip carpet and hard floors equally. On hardwood, tile, or laminate floors, footrests slide more easily — prioritize wide rubber base coverage for these surfaces. For carpeted floors, a wider base prevents the footrest from sinking or tipping on soft carpet fibers. Stability under dynamic use such as rocking and active movement requires either a wide stable base or a low center of gravity — check user reviews specifically for stability comments if you're buying a rocker model. Weight also contributes to stability: heavier footrests shift less but are harder to reposition throughout the day. Desk mat compatibility is worth considering if you use a floor mat under your chair. Many large chair mats extend forward under the desk, and a footrest sitting on the mat surface may slide more than one on bare flooring. On plastic mat surfaces, only footrests with deep non-slip rubber pads hold reliably. Some users place a strip of non-slip drawer liner under the footrest base as an extra measure on slippery surfaces. For standing desk users who frequently alternate between sitting and standing, a footrest that slides easily in and out from under the desk is more practical — in those cases, lighter-weight models with manageable bases are preferable to heavy, ultra-stable options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a footrest if I can touch the floor with my feet?

Only if your feet rest flat on the floor with your knees at approximately 90 degrees and thighs roughly parallel to the floor — if all three conditions are met simultaneously at your normal working position, you likely don't need a footrest. The problem arises when those conditions can't all be satisfied at once. If you raise your chair to reach the correct keyboard height, your feet may dangle short of the floor. If you lower your chair to plant your feet flat, the keyboard may be too high, forcing you to raise your shoulders and strain your wrists. Footrests are particularly essential for shorter users — anyone under approximately 5'4" using a standard-height desk (typically 28–30 inches) will almost certainly benefit. They are also useful for taller users with unusually high chairs configured around monitor height. The key test: sit at your normal working position with your monitor at eye level and your keyboard at a comfortable wrist height. If your feet are not fully flat on the floor in that position, a footrest is an ergonomic necessity, not a luxury. Even a minor gap of an inch or two forces subtle compensations throughout the kinetic chain — in the ankles, knees, hips, and lower back — that accumulate into real discomfort and strain across a full workday.

Can a footrest help with lower back pain?

Yes — when properly used, a footrest addresses one of the most common mechanical causes of desk-related lower back pain. The problem typically originates in hip angle: when your knees are at or above hip height, which happens when chair height is set too low or the seat pan is too deep, the lumbar spine flattens out. That loss of the natural lumbar curve places substantially more load on the discs and posterior muscles of the lower back, resulting in the familiar ache that builds over a long workday. A footrest raises your feet, allowing you to sit with hips slightly higher than or level with your knees. This tilt restores the anterior pelvic tilt that maintains the natural lumbar curve. Combined with a quality ergonomic chair with adjustable lumbar support, a footrest creates a complete foundation for healthy seated posture. The benefit is measurable: occupational therapy research on seated posture consistently shows that proper foot support reduces lower back muscle activation during sustained sitting, meaning those muscles work less to hold the spine upright. If you have an existing diagnosed back condition such as herniated disc, spondylosis, or spinal stenosis, consult an occupational therapist or physiotherapist for a comprehensive workstation assessment. A footrest is almost always part of that recommendation, but the specific height and angle may need to be tailored to your condition and symptoms.

How high should my footrest be?

Measure from the floor to just below your dangling feet when seated at your optimal keyboard and mouse height — that gap is your ideal footrest height. For most office workers using standard desks set at 28–30 inches, this measurement typically falls between 2 and 5 inches. Workers who are shorter or whose chairs are set particularly high to accommodate monitor positioning may need up to 6 inches of height. The measurement method: sit in your office chair and adjust to your normal working position — monitor at eye level, keyboard at wrist height, elbows at approximately 90 degrees. Now measure from the floor to the base of your heel. That number is your minimum footrest height requirement. If you want a footrest with a slight incline (which many users prefer), add 1–2 inches to account for the angle lifting the heel end higher. When positioned on the footrest, your feet should rest flat on the surface with your knees at approximately 90 degrees and thighs roughly parallel to the floor. The ankle angle should feel natural — not plantarflexed (toes pointing down) or heavily dorsiflexed (toes pulled up sharply). If you find yourself perching on the footrest, sliding your feet off the edge, or pressing your heels back against the footrest base, the height is not correctly matched to your chair and desk configuration. Opt for a model with multiple height settings so you can fine-tune after purchase.

Are rocking footrests better than static ones?

For circulation and long-term comfort during extended work sessions, rocking footrests offer a measurable advantage over static ones. The rocking motion engages the calf muscles in a gentle, rhythmic contraction-and-release cycle. This calf muscle pump action is the primary mechanism by which the body returns venous blood from the lower extremities back toward the heart — the same mechanism that makes walking beneficial for circulation. During completely static sitting, that pump is inactive, which contributes to the ankle swelling, leg heaviness, and foot fatigue that many desk workers experience by the end of the day. Active rocking footrests also reduce the unconscious fidgeting and foot tapping that people engage in when legs become uncomfortable — channeling that natural movement impulse into a controlled, beneficial motion. In open-plan offices, the smooth back-and-forth of a rocking footrest is less disruptive than irregular foot tapping. For users with balance concerns, vestibular conditions, or those who need precise foot control for pedal-based professional work — such as audio engineers using sustain pedals or video editors using jog wheels — a stable static footrest is the more appropriate choice. Static models also work better when the user wears high-heeled shoes, which already shift weight to the ball of the foot in a way that can become unstable on a rocker platform. Assess your specific use case and footwear before choosing between active rocker and static designs.

Can I use a footrest with a standing desk?

Yes — and the benefit applies in two distinct ways depending on whether the desk is in sitting or standing mode. When your standing desk is lowered to sitting height, a footrest improves seated leg ergonomics exactly as described throughout this guide: it fills the gap between the floor and your feet, maintains the correct knee angle, and supports lumbar posture. Height-adjustable desk users often configure their desk height for comfortable standing and then find it is too high when sitting — a footrest compensates for that gap rather than forcing constant desk-height adjustment throughout the day. When using the desk in standing mode, the dynamics change entirely. Standard under-desk footrests designed for seated use are not appropriate at standing height. What benefits standing users is an anti-fatigue mat, ideally one with varied surface topography — raised ridges, slopes, and contours that encourage subtle foot and ankle movement while standing. Products like the Topo Anti-Fatigue Mat include a central raised dome that lets you shift one foot up at a time, relieving lower back pressure during prolonged standing. If you frequently alternate between sitting and standing throughout the workday, consider purchasing both: a traditional adjustable footrest for seated ergonomics and a topographic anti-fatigue mat for standing ergonomics. Many standing desk users who adopt both report significantly reduced overall fatigue compared to using either alone, and the combined investment remains modest relative to the ergonomic benefit delivered across years of daily use.

How important is ergonomics when choosing home office equipment?

Ergonomics is the most important factor for home office equipment used for 4 or more hours per day, as discomfort and poor posture accumulate into musculoskeletal problems over months and years. OSHA and Mayo Clinic ergonomic guidelines identify the chair and desk height relationship as the most critical factor — forearms should be parallel to the floor when typing, with feet flat on the floor or a footrest. Monitor height should position the top of the screen at eye level or slightly below to prevent neck flexion. Investing in ergonomically sound primary equipment (chair, desk, monitor position) provides a higher return on health and productivity than any other home office upgrade.

What is the best way to set up a home office for productivity?

An effective home office setup prioritizes visual ergonomics, audio quality for calls, and lighting that minimizes eye strain. Position the primary monitor directly in front of you at arm's length, with the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level. Place task lighting to the left or right of the monitor (never behind or in front) to prevent glare and reflections. Use a dedicated headset or microphone and camera for video calls rather than laptop built-ins to project a professional presence. Separate your workspace visually from living areas when possible — a dedicated room significantly improves focus compared to working from a couch or dining table, even if only separated by a room divider.
