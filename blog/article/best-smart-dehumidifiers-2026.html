Best Smart Dehumidifiers of 2026

Smart dehumidifiers tackle mold, musty odors, and allergens while you control humidity remotely. We tested 8 models to find the best smart dehumidifiers for 2026.

By Alex Rivera ·May 8, 2026

Alex Rivera is a smart home specialist and IoT consultant with 7 years of experience. He has integrated and reviewed over 300 smart devices and helps readers build connected homes that actually work.

Smart dehumidifiers have transformed basement and crawlspace humidity management from a daily chore into a fully automated background process. The best models in 2026 connect to your home Wi-Fi network, integrate with Amazon Alexa and Google Home, and allow you to monitor and adjust target humidity levels from your smartphone regardless of whether you are upstairs or across the country. More importantly, they extract moisture more efficiently than their predecessors — a quality 50-pint smart dehumidifier can remove 50 pints of moisture per day from a 2,000-square-foot basement, measurably preventing mold growth when relative humidity stays below 50%. Our evaluation covered four buyer profiles: the homeowner managing a damp basement or crawlspace, the allergy sufferer needing precise humidity control in living areas, the property manager deploying dehumidifiers across multiple vacation rentals remotely, and the HVAC-conscious buyer looking for whole-home humidity management integration. Each unit was evaluated across moisture removal rate, energy efficiency (measured in liters per kilowatt-hour), noise level at low and high fan settings, smart app functionality, and drainage flexibility. This guide covers the six best smart dehumidifiers available on Amazon in 2026, along with a comprehensive buying guide addressing room coverage, energy efficiency ratings, drainage options, and smart home ecosystem compatibility. Whether you are fighting chronic basement dampness or managing seasonal humidity swings in a finished living space, the models here represent the best combination of performance, features, and value available this year.

Buying Guide

Pint Capacity and Room Coverage

Dehumidifier capacity is measured in pints of moisture removed per day under standard AHAM test conditions (80°F, 60% RH). The 2019 DOE test standard change means a unit rated at 20 pints today performs equivalently to a pre-2019 30-pint unit — account for this when comparing models across different years. For a 500–1,500 square foot finished basement with moderate humidity, a 20–30 pint unit is adequate. For 1,500–3,000 square feet or very high humidity above 70% RH, choose a 50-pint model. For post-flood drying or spaces above 3,000 square feet, a 70-pint unit like the Frigidaire or Honeywell is recommended. Oversizing by 10–15% is generally advisable — a slightly larger unit runs fewer hours to achieve target humidity, reducing wear and energy consumption over time.

Smart Connectivity and App Features

Smart dehumidifiers offer Wi-Fi app connectivity ranging from basic remote on/off to full automation integration. The most capable systems, like the Honeywell TP70WKN, support Amazon Alexa, Google Home, and Apple HomeKit simultaneously, enabling voice commands and integration with whole-home automation routines. The Midea Cube's SmartDry app provides real-time humidity graphs, usage history, and filter replacement reminders. Evaluate apps on three criteria: the clarity of real-time humidity display, the flexibility of scheduling options, and third-party platform integration depth. For vacation rental or remote property management, auto-restart after power outages and email/push notification alerts for full water tanks are particularly valuable features that distinguish capable smart dehumidifiers from basic Wi-Fi-enabled models.

Drainage Options: Tank vs. Continuous Drain

Dehumidifiers offer two drainage approaches: manual tank emptying and continuous drain hose connection. All models in this roundup include a removable water collection tank, typically holding 1.5 to 2.0 gallons, with an auto-shutoff when full. In high-humidity environments removing 50+ pints per day, tanks fill within 12–24 hours — requiring daily manual emptying unless a continuous drain option is used. The hOmeLabs 4,500 Sq. Ft model and Honeywell TP70WKN include a built-in pump for continuous drainage upward to a sink or window, the most convenient option. The Waykar and Frigidaire models include a gravity-drain hose outlet — suitable for routing to nearby floor drains without a pump. For any installation where manual tank emptying is impractical, prioritize models with continuous drain capability.

Energy Efficiency and Operating Costs

Dehumidifier energy efficiency is measured in liters of moisture removed per kilowatt-hour of electricity consumed. ENERGY STAR certified models meet a minimum of 1.85 L/kWh; ENERGY STAR Most Efficient models achieve 2.5+ L/kWh. The Midea Cube 20-pint leads this category at 2.56 L/kWh, saving $35–$50 per year versus baseline models. Operating costs add up over a dehumidification season — a 500-watt 50-pint unit running 8 hours per day at the U.S. average electricity rate of $0.16/kWh costs approximately $230 per year. An ENERGY STAR unit at equivalent performance consumes 15–30% less, saving $35–$70 annually. Over a 5–7 year dehumidifier lifespan, energy savings can more than offset a $30–$50 premium for an ENERGY STAR model versus a non-certified equivalent.

Noise Levels and Placement Considerations

Dehumidifier noise ranges from 45 dB at low fan speed (comparable to a quiet library) to 60 dB at high speed (comparable to a normal conversation). For placement in finished basement living spaces, bedrooms, or home offices, choose models rated at 50 dB or below at low fan speed — the Midea Cube 20 operates at approximately 46 dB in eco mode. For unfinished utility basements and crawlspaces where noise is not a concern, prioritize capacity and drainage over acoustics. The auto-humidity mode available on smart models allows the dehumidifier to run at low speed during occupied hours and switch to high speed during the night or when the space is empty, balancing noise and performance automatically. Units placed on concrete floors benefit from anti-vibration pads, which reduce transmitted floor resonance by 3–5 dB at high fan speeds.

Filter Maintenance and Long-Term Care

All dehumidifiers include an air filter that captures dust, pet dander, and mold spores as air circulates through the unit. Most manufacturers recommend cleaning the filter every two weeks in dusty environments and monthly in cleaner spaces. Smart models like the Midea Cube and Honeywell TP70WKN provide in-app filter maintenance reminders based on run hours. Neglecting the filter reduces airflow efficiency by 15–25% over time, measurably reducing moisture removal rates and increasing energy consumption. Cleaning is straightforward — remove the filter panel, rinse with warm water, and allow to dry completely before reinstallation. Annual deep cleaning of the coil fins with a soft brush maintains heat exchange efficiency. All models in this roundup use washable filters — there are no replacement filter consumable costs, unlike HEPA air purifiers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What humidity level should I set my smart dehumidifier to?

The EPA and ASHRAE recommend maintaining indoor relative humidity between 30% and 50% for comfort and mold prevention. Mold growth begins at sustained relative humidity above 60% — keeping your home below 50% RH provides a meaningful safety margin. For basements and crawlspaces where structural moisture damage is a concern, targeting 45–50% RH is appropriate. For main living areas during summer, 45–55% RH balances comfort with reasonable energy use. Smart dehumidifiers allow you to set a specific target humidity, and the unit will cycle on and off automatically to maintain it — a significant advantage over manual models that require frequent adjustments as outdoor humidity changes with weather patterns. In dry climates or during winter heating season, relative humidity may fall below 30%, at which point a humidifier rather than a dehumidifier is appropriate to prevent dry air discomfort, wood cracking, and static electricity buildup.

How much does it cost to run a smart dehumidifier?

Operating costs depend on the unit's wattage, daily run time, and local electricity rates. A 20-pint smart dehumidifier like the Midea Cube consumes approximately 300 watts at full speed. Running 8 hours per day at the U.S. average electricity rate of $0.16 per kilowatt-hour costs approximately $14 per month or $170 per year during a 12-month dehumidification season. A 50-pint model consuming 550 watts for the same runtime costs approximately $25 per month. ENERGY STAR certified models use 15–30% less energy than non-certified alternatives at equivalent moisture removal rates, saving $25–$70 annually. Smart dehumidifiers with auto-humidity mode reduce costs further by running only when humidity exceeds the target setpoint — in well-insulated homes that maintain low humidity naturally, the unit may only run 3–4 hours per day during drier periods, cutting operating costs by 40–50% compared to continuous operation.

Can a smart dehumidifier work in cold temperatures like an unheated garage?

Standard dehumidifiers are designed to operate between 41°F and 95°F. Below 41°F, the refrigerant coils begin to frost over, reducing moisture extraction efficiency and potentially damaging the compressor if ice buildup becomes severe. In an unheated garage, basement, or crawlspace that drops below 50°F in winter, choose a model with an auto-defrost function — the Honeywell TP70WKN and Frigidaire FFAD7033R1 both include automatic defrost that temporarily reverses the refrigeration cycle to melt coil frost before resuming normal operation. For spaces that consistently drop below 32°F, a standard refrigerant dehumidifier will not function and a desiccant dehumidifier designed for low-temperature operation is the appropriate solution. Most residential basements with any heating maintain temperatures above 50°F year-round, making standard models with auto-defrost adequate for most cold-climate basement applications.

How do I know if my home needs a dehumidifier?

Several signs indicate that a dehumidifier would meaningfully improve your home environment. Visible mold growth or mildew odors in basements, bathrooms, or closets indicates sustained high humidity above 60% RH. Condensation on windows during summer — when exterior humidity is high — suggests indoor humidity is also elevated. Warping or buckling wood floors, peeling paint or wallpaper, and rusting metal objects are physical indicators of chronic humidity damage. Allergy or asthma symptoms that worsen at home may be triggered by dust mites and mold, which thrive above 50% RH. The most reliable measurement is a digital hygrometer ($15–$25 on Amazon) placed in the affected space — if readings consistently exceed 55–60% RH, a dehumidifier will measurably improve air quality and protect building materials. A smart dehumidifier's real-time app monitoring provides ongoing humidity data to confirm that the unit is maintaining target levels in spaces you cannot constantly monitor.

What is the difference between a dehumidifier and an air purifier?

Dehumidifiers and air purifiers address different aspects of indoor air quality and should not be confused as interchangeable products. A dehumidifier extracts water vapor from the air using a refrigerant cooling coil, reducing relative humidity to prevent mold growth, dust mite proliferation, and moisture-related structural damage. It does not filter particles, gases, or odors — it only removes water vapor. An air purifier uses HEPA filters, activated carbon, and sometimes UV-C light to capture airborne particles including dust, pollen, pet dander, mold spores, bacteria, and VOCs — but it does not reduce humidity. For a complete indoor air quality solution in a damp basement, both products address different problems simultaneously: the dehumidifier eliminates the moisture conditions that allow mold and dust mites to thrive, while an air purifier captures the existing airborne spores and allergens already circulating. In very humid spaces, a dehumidifier is typically the higher-priority purchase, as reducing humidity prevents ongoing mold production rather than simply capturing it after the fact.

Do smart home devices work without internet?

Many smart home devices require internet connectivity for initial setup and cloud-based features, but local control capability varies significantly by brand and platform. Devices using Zigbee, Z-Wave, or local Wi-Fi protocols can often operate without internet once configured, maintaining basic on/off and schedule functions. Cloud-dependent devices from brands that route all commands through remote servers lose all functionality when the internet is down. Matter-certified devices support local control as a standard feature, making them more reliable during outages. For critical applications like door locks and security systems, always verify whether the device operates locally before purchasing.

Are smart home devices secure?

Smart home device security varies widely and requires active management by the user. Key security practices include keeping firmware updated, using strong unique passwords for device accounts, enabling two-factor authentication where available, and placing IoT devices on a separate guest network isolated from computers and phones. Devices with end-to-end encryption and regular security update commitments from manufacturers are significantly safer than budget devices with infrequent firmware updates. Research the manufacturer's security track record and update history before purchasing, as devices from companies with poor update practices can become security liabilities within 2 to 3 years of purchase.
